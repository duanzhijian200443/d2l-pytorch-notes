A volunteer group may use either consensus or one-person responsibility, depending on its purpose, size, and circumstances. Consensus works well when members share values, decisions are not urgent, and cooperation matters more than speed. It encourages participation, builds trust, and helps volunteers feel ownership of the outcome. However, it can become slow or produce vague compromises, especially in large or divided groups.

Assigning one person final responsibility works better when quick decisions, clear accountability, or specialized expertise are needed. A coordinator can act efficiently, resolve disagreements, and ensure that plans are implemented. This approach is particularly useful during emergencies or for projects with tight deadlines. Its danger is that volunteers may feel ignored or disengaged if the leader rarely consults them.

In practice, a hybrid model is often strongest. The group can discuss goals and major policies by consensus, while authorizing a leader or small committee to make routine or urgent decisions. The responsible person should explain decisions, invite feedback, and remain accountable to the whole group.
