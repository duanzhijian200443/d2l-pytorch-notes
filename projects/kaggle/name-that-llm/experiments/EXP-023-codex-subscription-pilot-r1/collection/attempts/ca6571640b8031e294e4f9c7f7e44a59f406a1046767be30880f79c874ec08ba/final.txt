Oral history preserves experiences that official records often overlook by recording people’s memories in their own words. Government files, institutional reports, and newspapers typically emphasize public decisions, prominent figures, or measurable events. Interviews can instead reveal how ordinary people experienced those events: how families coped with displacement, how workers understood workplace changes, or how marginalized communities maintained traditions despite discrimination. Personal accounts capture emotions, everyday routines, informal relationships, and meanings that rarely appear in administrative documents. They can also challenge dominant narratives by introducing perspectives excluded from institutions that produced and preserved written records.

However, researchers should recognize that memory is selective and shaped by time, later experiences, and the interview setting. A narrator may misremember dates, combine events, or interpret the past through present concerns. Researchers should therefore compare interviews with other accounts and available evidence, while recognizing that even factual inconsistencies can reveal how people understand their experiences. Oral history complements, rather than simply replaces, other historical sources.
