Historians often prefer several incomplete sources because no single account is fully neutral, comprehensive, or error-free. Each source reflects its creator’s position, purpose, audience, and access to information. A government report may record official decisions but omit dissent; a diary may reveal personal experience while being narrow or biased; archaeological evidence may show material conditions without explaining motives. By comparing these fragments, historians can identify agreements, contradictions, silences, and patterns. This process, called corroboration, helps test reliability and reduce dependence on one author’s perspective. Incomplete sources can also complement one another: one may supply dates, another voices, and another context. Rather than treating gaps as failures, historians use them carefully to build the most well-supported interpretation possible while remaining open about uncertainty.
