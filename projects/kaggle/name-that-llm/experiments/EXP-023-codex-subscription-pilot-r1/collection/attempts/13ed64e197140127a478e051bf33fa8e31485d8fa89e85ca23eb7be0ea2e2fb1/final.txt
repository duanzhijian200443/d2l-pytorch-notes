Remembering someone’s story is often more meaningful than remembering their name—it shows genuine interest. Still, names matter, and you can build a simple system to support your memory.

When you meet someone, repeat their name naturally: “Nice to meet you, Maya. How did you get involved with this group?” Link the name to one distinctive detail from their story, such as “Maya—new gardener” or “Carlos—training for a marathon.” Avoid relying on appearance-based associations; use context and conversation instead.

If appropriate, make a quick private note after the interaction: name, where you met, and one or two details they shared. Before the next event, glance over those notes.

When you forget, be direct and warm rather than pretending: “I’m sorry—I remember you told me about your move, but I’m blanking on your name. Could you remind me?” Most people respond kindly, especially when you recall something meaningful about them.

You can also reduce pressure by asking for names early in a conversation: “Please remind me of your name—I want to make sure I get it right.” Then repeat it once more.

Finally, give yourself grace. Names are arbitrary labels; stories carry connection. Your habit of remembering people’s experiences is already a strong foundation for making others feel seen.
