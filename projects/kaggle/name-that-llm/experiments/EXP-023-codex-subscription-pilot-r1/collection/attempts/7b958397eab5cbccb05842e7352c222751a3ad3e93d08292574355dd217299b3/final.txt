Every evening, Mara practiced the violin in the old concert hall after everyone had gone home. Rows of empty velvet seats disappeared into shadow, and the chandeliers hung unlit above her. She liked the silence there. It made every missed note impossible to ignore.

For weeks, she worked on the same difficult passage, stopping whenever her bow scraped or her fingers stumbled. Yet sometimes, after she finished, she noticed small changes: a door left slightly open, the faint scent of lemon polish, a bucket rolling somewhere backstage.

One night, frustrated, Mara lowered her violin and called, “Is someone there?”

A cleaner stepped from behind the last row, holding a mop. He looked embarrassed.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “I didn’t mean to disturb you.”

“You’ve been listening?”

He nodded. “Every evening. My wife loved that piece. I haven’t heard it since she died.”

Mara looked at the empty seats, then at the man’s tired face. She raised the violin again.

“This time,” she said, “sit closer.”

He chose a seat in the front row. Mara began from the beginning. She still missed one note, then another, but she didn’t stop. When the final sound faded, the cleaner was smiling through tears.

For the first time, the hall did not feel empty.
