Each evening, after the conservatory emptied and the last door clicked shut, Mara returned to the recital hall with her violin.

The room was too large for her mistakes. A missed shift rose into the balcony; a thin note trembled beneath the gilt ceiling. She practiced until the clocks in the corridor struck nine, playing the difficult passage again and again—slower, cleaner, braver.

Near closing, she always heard the soft scrape of a mop beyond the side entrance. She assumed the cleaner was simply working.

One rainy Thursday, Mara stopped midway through the piece. The hall held its breath.

From the shadows came a quiet voice. “The second phrase wants to lean forward.”

Mara turned. The cleaner stood beside his cart, one hand on its handle, embarrassed by his own interruption.

“You know it?” she asked.

He smiled. “My wife played it. Every night, you get closer to how she did.”

He told her he had been listening for weeks, pausing outside the doors while polishing the lobby floors. Mara lifted her violin again, suddenly less alone in the vast room.

“Will you stay?” she asked.

The cleaner parked his cart in the front row.

This time, when Mara began, she played not for empty seats, but for the one listener who had heard every imperfect note—and waited for the music to become whole.
