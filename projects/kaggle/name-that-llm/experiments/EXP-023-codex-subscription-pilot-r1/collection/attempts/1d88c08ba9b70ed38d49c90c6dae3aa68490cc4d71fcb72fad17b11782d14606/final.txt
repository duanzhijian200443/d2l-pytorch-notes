A second confirmation can help when the action is genuinely irreversible or high impact: deleting an account, permanently erasing records, revoking production access, or sending a large payment. It creates a brief pause that interrupts automatic clicking and gives users a chance to notice mistakes, especially when the first action may have been triggered accidentally. The second step is most useful if it adds meaningful information—for example, stating what will be deleted, how many items are affected, whether recovery is possible, and who may be impacted.

Repeated confirmation becomes ineffective when users encounter it routinely. People learn that dialogs are merely obstacles and dismiss them without reading, a phenomenon often called “click-through” or alert fatigue. Adding several identical “Are you sure?” prompts does not improve comprehension; it trains faster confirmation.

The design should therefore match friction to risk. Use one clear confirmation for ordinary destructive actions, with undo or a recovery window where feasible. Reserve stronger measures for exceptional risk: requiring the user to type a name, reauthenticate, review a summary, or obtain another person’s approval. Avoid confirmations for actions that are easy to reverse, since unnecessary interruptions damage flow and reduce the credibility of warnings that truly matter.
