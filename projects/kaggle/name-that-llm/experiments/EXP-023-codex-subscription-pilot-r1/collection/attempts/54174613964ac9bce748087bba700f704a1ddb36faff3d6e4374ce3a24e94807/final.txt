The postcard showed the lighthouse before its windows were replaced, before the breakwater, before anyone thought to paint the door red.

On the back, in careful capitals, someone had written:

PLEASE KEEP YOUR LIGHT ON NEXT THURSDAY. I AM BRINGING HIM HOME.

Mara checked the postmark. A town three hundred miles inland. No signature.

Thursday arrived with rain and a minibus.

An elderly man stepped out holding a biscuit tin. His daughter followed, apologizing before she reached the door.

“He worked here in 1962. He wanted to write ahead.”

The man ignored them both. He put his palm against the stone.

“Still cold,” he said.

Inside, Mara made tea. He opened the tin: brass screws, a payroll slip, and a photograph of two young keepers grinning over a burnt saucepan.

“Your predecessor owes me six shillings.”

“Interest might bankrupt us,” Mara said.

At dusk, she took him upstairs. He watched the lamp begin its sweep.

His daughter quietly folded the unused wheelchair back into the bus.
