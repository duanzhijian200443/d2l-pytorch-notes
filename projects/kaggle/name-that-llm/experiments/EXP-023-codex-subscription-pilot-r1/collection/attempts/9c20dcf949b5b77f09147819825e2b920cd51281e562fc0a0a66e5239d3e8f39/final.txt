The claim is too absolute. A good explanation should avoid unnecessary technical vocabulary, but it should not always avoid it.

Technical terms can make an explanation more precise and efficient. For example, in medicine, “hypertension” identifies a specific condition more clearly than a long informal description of persistently high blood pressure. In programming, terms such as “recursion” or “encryption” let informed readers connect an explanation to established ideas, methods, and documentation.

The key is audience and purpose. For beginners, unexplained jargon can create confusion and make a subject seem inaccessible. A strong explanation may introduce the plain-language idea first, then give the technical term: “This is a way for a function to call itself; programmers call it recursion.” That approach preserves clarity while building the reader’s vocabulary.

Avoiding all technical language can also be misleading. Everyday substitutes may be vague, inaccurate, or cumbersome, especially when distinctions matter. Calling every online security measure a “password protection system,” for instance, obscures differences between authentication, authorization, and encryption.

Therefore, good explanations use technical vocabulary deliberately: omit it when it adds no value, define it when the audience may not know it, and retain it when precision or future learning requires it.
