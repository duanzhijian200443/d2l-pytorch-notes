A damp cloth dries as liquid water evaporates from its fibers into the surrounding air. Spreading the cloth out exposes a much larger surface area, allowing more water molecules to escape into the air at the same time. Both sides can contribute if air can reach them.

When the cloth is rolled tightly, much of its wet surface is hidden between layers. Water inside the roll must move toward an exposed surface, or evaporate into small air spaces and then diffuse outward. These processes slow drying.

The surrounding air also matters. Air next to a wet cloth becomes humid as it gains water vapor. If that humid air remains in place, net evaporation slows because the air is closer to saturation. Moving air carries water vapor away and replaces it with drier air, maintaining faster evaporation. A spread-out cloth allows this air exchange more easily than a tight roll. Thus, greater exposed surface area and better ventilation work together to speed drying.
