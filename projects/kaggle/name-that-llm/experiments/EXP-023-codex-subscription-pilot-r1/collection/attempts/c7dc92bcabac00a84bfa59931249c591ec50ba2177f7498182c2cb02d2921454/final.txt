Standardized units made trade easier by giving buyers and sellers a shared way to measure weight, length, volume, and value. A merchant could trust that a kilogram of grain or a meter of cloth meant roughly the same thing in another town, reducing disputes, fraud, and the time needed to negotiate. Common measures also supported wider markets, taxation, record-keeping, and transport.

Yet the disappearance of local measures could mean losing cultural knowledge and practices. Traditional units often reflected local landscapes, crops, labor, and everyday life. They carried regional language, history, and practical experience—for example, measures based on a field’s size or a container used in local farming. Standardization improved exchange, but sometimes weakened local identity and flexibility.
