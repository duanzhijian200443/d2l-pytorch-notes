Ocean tides occur because the Moon’s gravity pulls unevenly on Earth. The side facing the Moon feels a slightly stronger gravitational force than Earth’s center, producing a bulge of water toward the Moon. At the same time, the side facing away experiences weaker lunar gravity than Earth’s center. As Earth and the Moon orbit their common center of mass, the whole Earth is continually accelerating in orbit; relative to that motion, water on the far side forms a second bulge outward.

Earth’s rotation carries coastlines through these two bulges, producing generally two high tides and two low tides each day. The water does not simply collect permanently on the Moon-facing side because Earth is rotating, the gravitational force varies across the planet rather than acting only in one direction, and the ocean is part of a moving, rotating system. In addition, continents, ocean-basin shapes, seafloor depth, and friction redirect and delay the tidal motion, so actual tides differ greatly from place to place. The Sun also contributes tides, though its effect is weaker than the Moon’s because it is much farther away.
