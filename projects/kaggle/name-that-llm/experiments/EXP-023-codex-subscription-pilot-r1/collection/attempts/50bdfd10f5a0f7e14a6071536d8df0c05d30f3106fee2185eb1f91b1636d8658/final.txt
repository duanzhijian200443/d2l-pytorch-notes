A map is a selective interpretation of a place because it cannot reproduce every feature, experience, or relationship that exists there. Instead, its maker chooses what to include, what to omit, and how to present information. Those choices reflect the map’s purpose, intended audience, available data, and sometimes political or cultural assumptions.

Scale determines which details are visible: a national map may show major highways while leaving out footpaths and small communities. Symbols, colors, and labels also shape what viewers notice. A thick boundary line can make a border appear fixed and unquestionable, even when it is disputed. Place names may privilege one language or historical perspective over another.

Even the geometry of a world map involves interpretation. Flattening Earth’s curved surface inevitably distorts some combination of area, shape, distance, or direction. Different projections therefore produce different impressions of the relative size and importance of regions.

Maps also struggle to convey lived experience: a street may look like a simple route while feeling welcoming, dangerous, or inaccessible to different people.

This does not mean maps are necessarily inaccurate or deceptive. Their usefulness depends on simplification. But reading a map critically means asking whose perspective it represents, what purpose it serves, and what it leaves invisible.
