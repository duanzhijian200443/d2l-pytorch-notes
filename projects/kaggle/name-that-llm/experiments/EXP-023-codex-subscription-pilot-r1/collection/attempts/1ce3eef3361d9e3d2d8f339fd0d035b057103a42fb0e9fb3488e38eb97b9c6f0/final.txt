Choosing Alex and Bea gives the same pair as choosing Bea and Alex because the committee is selecting a group, not assigning different roles. The order in which the two people are named does not change who belongs to the group. In symbols, the pair {Alex, Bea} is identical to {Bea, Alex}. These are therefore counted only once.

Suppose the four people are Alex, Bea, Carlos, and Dana. The possible pairs are:

1. Alex and Bea  
2. Alex and Carlos  
3. Alex and Dana  
4. Bea and Carlos  
5. Bea and Dana  
6. Carlos and Dana  

Another way to count is to start with ordered choices. There are four choices for the first person and three for the second, giving \(4 \times 3 = 12\). However, every pair appears twice: Alex–Bea and Bea–Alex, for example. Dividing by two gives \(12/2 = 6\). Equivalently, the combination formula gives \(\binom{4}{2} = 6\). Thus, the committee has **six distinct possible pairs**.
