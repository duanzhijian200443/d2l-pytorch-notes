A damp cloth dries as water molecules escape from its surface into the air. When the cloth is spread out, much more wet surface is exposed, so more water can evaporate at the same time. In a tightly rolled cloth, many wet fibers and layers are hidden inside, where their water has little direct contact with air.

The surrounding air is equally important. Air immediately next to wet material quickly becomes humid. If that moist air remains trapped—as it does inside a roll—evaporation slows because the air cannot readily accept much more water vapor. Around a spread-out cloth, fresh, drier air can reach the surface more easily, while humid air can move away.

Air movement, such as wind or a fan, strengthens this effect by continually replacing the humid boundary layer with drier air. Warm, dry air generally speeds drying further, whereas cool or already-humid air slows it. Thus, spreading the cloth improves both exposed surface area and ventilation.
