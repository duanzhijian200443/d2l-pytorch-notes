The postcard shows the lighthouse from the sea, painted in faded blues. On the back, in neat block letters, it reads:

“Thank you for keeping watch. I got home.”

Mara, the keeper, studies the stamp: forty years old. No return address. She checks the visitor log and finds an entry from that summer—a twelve-year-old boy separated from his family during a foggy afternoon. The previous keeper had guided him toward the harbor with the light’s beam and a hand-cranked foghorn.

Mara takes the postcard to the village café. An elderly fisherman recognizes the handwriting immediately. His brother had been that boy. He had moved abroad, raised a family, and died the previous winter.

The fisherman smiles, not sadly. “He always said that light saved him.”

Together they frame the postcard and hang it in the lighthouse’s small museum. After that, visitors leave their own notes: apologies, gratitude, directions they once needed.

At dusk, Mara climbs the stairs, lights the lamp, and adds one line to the log:

“Still here. Still watching.”
