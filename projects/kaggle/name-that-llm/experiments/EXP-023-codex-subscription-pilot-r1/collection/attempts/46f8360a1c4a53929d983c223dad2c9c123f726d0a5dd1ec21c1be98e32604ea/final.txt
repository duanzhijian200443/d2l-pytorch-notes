Explaining a historical person’s choices means examining the pressures, beliefs, institutions, and available alternatives that shaped their decisions. It asks why an action seemed reasonable, necessary, or advantageous to that person at that time. Excusing every consequence goes further: it treats those circumstances as removing responsibility or making the resulting harm acceptable.

Context should deepen judgment, not replace it. We can recognize that someone inherited prejudices or faced severe constraints while still asking whether contemporaries challenged those prejudices, whether less harmful options existed, and whose suffering they ignored. We should also distinguish foreseeable harms from unintended outcomes they could not reasonably anticipate. Historical understanding therefore allows both empathy and criticism: a choice can be understandable without being justified, and consequential without being wholly foreseeable.
