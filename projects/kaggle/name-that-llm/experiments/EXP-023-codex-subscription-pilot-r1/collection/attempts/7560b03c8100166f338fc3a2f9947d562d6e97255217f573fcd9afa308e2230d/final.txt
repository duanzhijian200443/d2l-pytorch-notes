Knowing the shelf holds either eight large boxes or twelve small boxes gives two separate capacity limits, but it does not specify how large and small boxes combine. We need an additional relationship between their sizes—for example, how many small-box spaces equal one large-box space. If one large box occupies the same room as two small boxes, the shelf could hold combinations satisfying \(2L+S\leq16\), where \(L\) and \(S\) are the numbers of large and small boxes. But if a large box equals three small boxes, the rule would instead be \(3L+S\leq24\). Both relationships are consistent with the stated capacities, yet they allow different mixtures. Therefore, the two endpoint capacities alone do not determine the mixed-size capacity.
