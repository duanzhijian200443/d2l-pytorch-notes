The first notes drifted from the laundromat’s radio while Mara waited for the dryer to stop thumping. She knew them at once: a bright little piano intro from the song her father used to hum while making Sunday pancakes.

But the melody moved strangely, dragging its feet. The singer’s voice had sunk low and syrupy, every cheerful phrase stretched until it sounded mournful. Someone had set the player to the wrong speed.

Mara laughed before she could stop herself. Then the laugh caught.

She remembered being eight, sitting at the kitchen table with a fever, furious because she was missing a school trip. Her father had played that same cassette too slowly, pretending it was a “sleepy version” made especially for sick children. He had brought her toast cut into stars, though he always burned toast, and sat beside her all afternoon, humming along in a ridiculous baritone.

At the time, she had only complained that he was ruining her favorite song.

The dryer chimed. Mara folded her warm shirts carefully, blinking at the fluorescent lights. Outside, she called her father—not to apologize exactly, because there was no way to make one small kindness carry all it had become—but to ask whether he still knew the sleepy version.
