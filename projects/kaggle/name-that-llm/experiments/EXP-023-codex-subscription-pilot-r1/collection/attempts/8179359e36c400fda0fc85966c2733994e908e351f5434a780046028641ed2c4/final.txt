At seven-thirty, just as the dining room filled with the clink of glasses and the smell of rosemary chicken, the lights went out.

For one stunned second, Mara, the owner, heard only the refrigerator fans winding down. Then phones appeared, their screens casting pale islands across familiar faces. She found candles in the storeroom, and within minutes the restaurant glowed softly instead of brightly.

Without the register, orders became handwritten. Without the kitchen’s electric grill, the chef served cold antipasti, bread, and whatever could be finished over the gas burners. Mara apologized table by table.

“No need,” said Mr. Alvarez, who came every Thursday. “We know what you keep in the pantry.”

Soon regulars were carrying ice from the bar, translating the handwritten menu, entertaining children, and helping servers remember who had ordered what. One customer even knew the location of the emergency flashlight.

By closing time, Mara understood something she had missed during ordinary evenings: her regulars did not simply return for dinner. They knew the place, cared for it, and regarded it as partly their own.
