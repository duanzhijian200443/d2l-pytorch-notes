Familiar rituals often survive because they acquire new functions after their original purpose fades. A practice that began for religious, practical, or historical reasons may later provide comfort, predictability, identity, or social cohesion. Repetition makes it feel natural: people learn the ritual from family or community and continue it without needing an explicit explanation.

Rituals also carry emotional weight. They can connect participants to ancestors, mark important transitions, and create a sense of belonging. Abandoning them may feel disrespectful or unsettling, even when no one remembers why they began. Communities may also invent explanations afterward, giving an old custom a new symbolic meaning.

In addition, rituals reduce uncertainty. Familiar sequences tell people what to do at weddings, funerals, holidays, or moments of crisis, sparing them from having to improvise. Their survival therefore does not depend only on the original reason. A ritual persists when the act itself becomes useful—as a source of stability, shared memory, and collective identity.
