At seven thirty, the orchestra was ready, but the conductor refused to begin.

“One chair,” the stage manager whispered into her headset. “How do we lose one chair?”

There were spare chairs backstage. There were sixty empty seats in the balcony. The conductor rejected them all.

Mara, the newest usher, found the missing chair beside the loading dock, holding a door open. It was an ugly wooden thing with a split vinyl cushion and two inches sawn off each leg.

She carried it through the wings, expecting someone important to complain.

Instead, the principal cellist stood up.

“Thank you,” he said.

Only when he sat did Mara notice the brace beneath his trouser leg. A standard chair left his injured foot dangling; after ten minutes, the pressure became unbearable.

“Couldn’t you have asked for a footstool?” she whispered.

“We tried one at rehearsal. It slipped.”

He settled his cello. The conductor raised her baton.

Mara went back to the loading dock and found a proper doorstop.
