The invention of printing did not instantly make handwritten books and documents unnecessary because printing served some needs better than others. Early presses required expensive equipment, skilled workers, and considerable preparation. Setting type was worthwhile when many copies were needed, but handwriting remained cheaper and quicker for a single letter, legal agreement, account entry, or short-lived notice.

Manuscripts also retained cultural and practical value. Wealthy patrons continued to commission beautifully handwritten and illuminated books as objects of prestige. Scholars and students copied texts, annotated printed volumes, and circulated ideas privately in handwritten form. Some works attracted too few readers to justify a print run, while others were kept out of print because of censorship or sensitive content.

Moreover, printing spread unevenly. Many communities lacked nearby presses, and established habits and occupations did not disappear overnight. Printed books therefore supplemented handwritten materials long before they displaced them in particular settings. Rather than an immediate replacement, the change involved centuries of coexistence and adaptation.
