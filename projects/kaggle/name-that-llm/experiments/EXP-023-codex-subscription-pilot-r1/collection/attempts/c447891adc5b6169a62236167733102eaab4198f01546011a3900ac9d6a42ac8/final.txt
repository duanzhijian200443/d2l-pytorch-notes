A local history project should prioritize a process that involves many residents, while still aiming for a polished final exhibition. The exhibition matters because it gives the project visibility, preserves its materials, and offers the community a clear and meaningful outcome. However, a beautiful display created by only a few people may present history as something finished and authoritative, rather than something shaped by diverse memories and perspectives.

Broad participation can uncover stories that formal archives overlook, including personal experiences, family traditions, migration histories, and disagreements about the past. It also builds trust and gives residents a sense of ownership. Workshops, interviews, collection days, and opportunities to review or challenge interpretations can make the project more inclusive and historically responsible.

This does not mean sacrificing quality. Curators can provide structure, edit materials carefully, and design an engaging exhibition while ensuring that residents influence its themes and content. The final display might even include the research process itself: recordings, unanswered questions, conflicting memories, and explanations of how objects were selected.

Ultimately, the exhibition should be understood as the public expression of a participatory process. If forced to choose, meaningful resident involvement should come first, because it produces a richer, more representative history and leaves behind stronger community relationships.
