A proverb does more than describe a scene: it uses a familiar image to offer advice, make a judgment, or warn against a mistake. Translating it word for word may preserve the scene while losing what speakers conventionally mean by invoking it.

Its practical meaning depends partly on shared cultural knowledge. Animals, foods, occupations, and customs can carry associations that are obvious in one community but unfamiliar in another. Readers may understand every translated word yet fail to recognize the lesson—or draw a different lesson from the same image.

For example, the English proverb “Don’t count your chickens before they hatch” is not usually advice about poultry. It warns against treating an uncertain outcome as guaranteed. A literal translation preserves chickens and eggs, but unless readers recognize the metaphor, its relevance to a promised promotion or an expected profit may remain unclear.

Proverbs also perform social actions: they can gently criticize, reassure, caution, or end an argument. Familiar wording helps listeners identify that function immediately. An unfamiliar literal rendering may sound puzzling rather than persuasive.

Effective translation therefore considers not only “What picture do these words create?” but also “What is the speaker trying to accomplish?” Sometimes a culturally equivalent proverb—or a brief explanation—preserves that purpose better than identical imagery.
