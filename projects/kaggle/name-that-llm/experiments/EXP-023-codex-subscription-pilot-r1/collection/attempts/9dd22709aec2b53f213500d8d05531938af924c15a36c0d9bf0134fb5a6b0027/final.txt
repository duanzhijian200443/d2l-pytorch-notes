Oral history preserves personal experiences that official records often omit: family life, workplace culture, migration, discrimination, community rituals, emotions, and everyday responses to major events. Interviews can bring forward the perspectives of people rarely represented in government files, newspapers, or institutional archives, including workers, women, Indigenous communities, migrants, and political minorities. A narrator may explain how a policy, war, strike, or environmental change affected ordinary routines, relationships, and choices—details that statistics or formal reports cannot convey. Oral testimony can also preserve local knowledge and language before they disappear.

Researchers should recognize, however, that memory is not a neutral recording. Recollections may change over time, be shaped by later events, omit painful material, or reflect a narrator’s desire to present themselves or others in a particular light. Rather than treating this simply as error, historians can analyze why an account is remembered and told in a certain way. They should corroborate testimony where possible with other interviews and documentary evidence.
