Offering visitors a detailed audio guide provides structure, context, and historical depth. It can explain architectural features, introduce important figures, and connect individual locations to broader events. This is especially valuable at complex or unfamiliar sites, helping visitors notice details they might otherwise overlook. Audio guides can also support different languages and accessibility needs, allowing people to explore independently while still receiving expert interpretation. However, a detailed narration may encourage visitors to follow a fixed route and focus on the guide rather than their surroundings. It can make the experience feel less spontaneous and may overwhelm those who prefer quiet observation.

Encouraging visitors to explore without narration creates a more personal and flexible experience. People can move at their own pace, form their own impressions, and appreciate the atmosphere, scale, and physical evidence of the site. This approach may inspire curiosity and reflection, particularly in places where emotional or sensory experience is as important as factual information. Its drawback is that visitors may miss significant details or misunderstand the site’s historical meaning. Those with limited prior knowledge may also feel uncertain about what they are seeing.

The best approach may combine both options: offer a concise introductory guide and optional detailed content, while clearly inviting visitors to pause, look around, and explore independently.
