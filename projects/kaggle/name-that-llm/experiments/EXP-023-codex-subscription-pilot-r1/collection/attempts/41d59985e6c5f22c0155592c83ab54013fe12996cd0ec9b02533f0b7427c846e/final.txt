A river’s path describes the overall route that flowing water follows through the landscape: from its source downhill through channels, bends, tributaries, floodplains, and eventually to a lake, sea, or another river. This path is shaped by gravity, terrain, erosion, sediment deposition, and obstacles. It is a large-scale, visible feature that can remain recognizable for years, even though the water within it is constantly replaced.

An individual water molecule behaves differently. It generally travels downstream with the bulk flow, but its exact motion is not a smooth line matching the river channel. Turbulence, friction with the riverbed and banks, eddies, mixing, and collisions with other molecules cause it to move irregularly in three dimensions. A molecule may swirl sideways, briefly move upstream within an eddy, sink or rise, become trapped near sediment or vegetation, or evaporate before reaching the river’s end.

So, the river path is the organized, macroscopic route of the flow, while a molecule’s trajectory is a complex, microscopic and often unpredictable journey within that route.
