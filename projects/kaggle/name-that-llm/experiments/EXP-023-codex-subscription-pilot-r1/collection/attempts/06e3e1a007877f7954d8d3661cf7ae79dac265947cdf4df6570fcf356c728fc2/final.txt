Before electronic communication became common, public squares served as the social heart of a town. They brought together people who might otherwise have remained separated by occupation, neighborhood, or social rank. Residents gathered there to buy and sell goods, hear official announcements, exchange news, attend religious or civic ceremonies, and watch public entertainments. Because information traveled mainly by word of mouth, the square functioned as an informal communication network where rumors, political opinions, job opportunities, and family news circulated rapidly.

Public squares also helped create a shared local identity. Markets, festivals, processions, protests, and celebrations allowed townspeople to experience important events collectively. Simply spending time in the square—talking, observing strangers, meeting friends, or negotiating business—strengthened social ties and made community life visible. At the same time, squares could reinforce authority, since governments used them for proclamations, punishments, and displays of power. Thus, the public square combined the roles of marketplace, news center, meeting place, stage, and civic forum.
