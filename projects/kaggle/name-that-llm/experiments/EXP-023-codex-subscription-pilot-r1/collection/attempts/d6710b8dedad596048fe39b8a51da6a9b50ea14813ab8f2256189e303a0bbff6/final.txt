Familiar rituals often survive because they become meaningful in their own right, even after their original purpose disappears. Repetition makes them feel natural and comforting, while shared participation turns them into symbols of belonging. A ritual may begin as a practical response to danger, religion, hygiene, or social organization, but later generations inherit only the action, not the explanation. The practice can then be maintained because it connects people to family, community, or the past.

Rituals also reduce uncertainty. Performing a known sequence can provide a sense of order during transitions, celebrations, or crises, even when no one fully believes in its original rationale. Social pressure matters too: abandoning a custom may seem disrespectful, risky, or disloyal. In addition, rituals are often adaptable. Their meaning can shift while their visible form remains stable, allowing them to serve new emotional or social purposes.

Thus, forgotten reasons do not necessarily weaken rituals. Familiarity, identity, memory, and emotional reassurance can become stronger justifications than the original one.
