A map is a selective interpretation because it cannot include every feature, perspective, or experience of a place. Its maker must choose what to show, omit, emphasize, name, classify, and symbolize. A road map may foreground highways and exits while leaving out neighborhood boundaries, cultural sites, or environmental conditions. A political map may draw national borders prominently, even where those borders are disputed or experienced differently by local communities.

Scale also requires selection: a city map can show individual streets, while a world map must simplify coastlines, settlements, and terrain. Symbols and colors further shape meaning. A thick red line can make a border appear firm and important; shading can imply risk, wealth, density, or control. Even projection choices matter, since flattening the curved Earth necessarily distorts area, distance, or shape.

Maps are often created for particular purposes—navigation, taxation, military planning, tourism, resource extraction, or public health—and those purposes influence what counts as relevant information. They may reflect the assumptions and power of institutions that fund or use them.

This does not mean maps are useless or simply biased. They can represent geographic evidence accurately and be extremely valuable. But they are models, not the territory itself: structured arguments about a place, designed to help particular users see some relationships more clearly than others.
