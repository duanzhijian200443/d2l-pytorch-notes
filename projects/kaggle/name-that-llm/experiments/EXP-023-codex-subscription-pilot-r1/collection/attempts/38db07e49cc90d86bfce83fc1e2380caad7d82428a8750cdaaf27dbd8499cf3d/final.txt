An unreliable narrator makes readers active participants because the story cannot be accepted at face value. Instead of simply receiving events and judgments, readers must question what the narrator reports, notice contradictions, and compare words with actions or other characters’ perspectives. This uncertainty creates a kind of interpretive detective work: readers construct possible versions of reality and decide which details deserve trust. The narrator’s biases, limited knowledge, emotional instability, or deliberate deception can also reveal themes indirectly, showing how memory, guilt, prejudice, or self-protection shape experience. As a result, meaning is not handed down by an authoritative voice; it is negotiated between the text and the reader. The pleasure of reading comes partly from discovering not only what happened, but how and why it was distorted.
