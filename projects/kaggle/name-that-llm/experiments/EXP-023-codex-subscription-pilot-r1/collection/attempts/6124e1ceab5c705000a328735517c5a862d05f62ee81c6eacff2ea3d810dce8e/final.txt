A map is a selective interpretation because it cannot represent every feature of a place at full detail. Mapmakers must decide what to include, omit, emphasize, or simplify based on the map’s purpose, audience, scale, and available data. A subway map, for example, highlights routes and stations while distorting geographic distances; a political map emphasizes borders and territories; a tourist map foregrounds attractions and transportation.

Maps also reflect choices about symbols, colors, labels, projection, and orientation. These design decisions influence how viewers understand importance, proximity, connection, and authority. Even the boundaries shown on a map can reflect political perspectives or contested claims rather than universally accepted facts. The data used may contain gaps, biases, or outdated information, further shaping the representation.

Scale is especially important: a large-scale map can show individual buildings and streets, while a small-scale map must generalize them. Consequently, every map constructs a particular view of reality rather than reproducing reality itself. It is best understood as an argument or model about a place—useful for certain purposes, but never complete or entirely neutral.
