Choose a calm moment—not while either of you is waiting—and frame it as a shared logistics issue rather than a flaw. You might say: “I’ve noticed you often arrive about twenty minutes early, and I’m usually there at the planned time. I worry you may be left waiting, and I’d like us to find a plan that feels good for both of us.”

Ask what being early means to them. They may feel anxious about lateness, prefer a buffer for travel, or simply enjoy settling in. Then explain your side without defensiveness: arriving at the agreed time is how you plan, not a sign that you value them less.

Offer practical options: set the meeting time twenty minutes later for them, agree that the earlier person will bring something to do, or send a message if either person expects to be more than a few minutes off schedule. Avoid “you always” or “you’re making me…” language. The goal is not to decide whose habit is correct, but to make expectations explicit and preserve goodwill.
