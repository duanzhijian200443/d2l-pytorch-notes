By sunset, Mara had taken ninety-three photographs of the harbor. Even on her camera’s tiny screen, she could tell they were her best yet: gulls suspended above silver water, fishing nets glowing copper, a boy leaping between two shadows.

At home, she deleted crooked horizons and duplicate boats. Then she reached the last photograph.

It was hopeless. The pier slanted sideways. The sunset had become a smear of orange. Across the foreground floated something pale and shapeless.

Her father’s hand.

He had come along reluctantly, complaining about his knees, carrying the camera bag anyway. While she waited for the light, he bought them paper cups of tea.

“Look at that,” he’d said.

She turned with the camera still raised. He was pointing toward a seal beside the pilings, grinning with an expression she remembered from childhood. She pressed the shutter as he nudged her shoulder.

Neither his face nor the seal appeared in the photograph.

Mara’s finger hovered over Delete.

Then she made a folder called Harbor, selected twelve sharp images, and added the blurred one.

Months later, when her father asked for a print of the boats, she ordered two photographs.

The boats went into his hallway.

The other stayed beside her desk.
