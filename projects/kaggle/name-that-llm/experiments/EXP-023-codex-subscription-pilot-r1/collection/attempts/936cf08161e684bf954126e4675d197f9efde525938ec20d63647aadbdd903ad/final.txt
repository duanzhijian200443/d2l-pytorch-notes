Choosing Alex and Bea is the same pair as choosing Bea and Alex because the committee is selecting a group, not assigning different positions. Both choices contain exactly the same two people. Changing the order in which their names are written does not change who represents the committee.

To count the possible pairs, call the four people Alex, Bea, Chris, and Dana. The distinct pairs are:

- Alex and Bea
- Alex and Chris
- Alex and Dana
- Bea and Chris
- Bea and Dana
- Chris and Dana

Therefore, there are **6 possible pairs**.

Another way to count is to choose one person first and another person second. There are 4 choices for the first person and 3 for the second, giving \(4 \times 3 = 12\). However, this counts every pair twice: once in each order. Dividing by 2 corrects the double counting:

\[
\frac{4 \times 3}{2}=\boxed{6}.
\]

This is a **combination**, where order does not matter.
