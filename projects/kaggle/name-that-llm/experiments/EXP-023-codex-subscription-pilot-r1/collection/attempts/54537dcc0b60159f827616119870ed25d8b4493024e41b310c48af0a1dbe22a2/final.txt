Floating ice displaces an amount of water equal to its own weight. This is Archimedes’ principle.

Before melting, the ice pushes aside enough liquid water to support its weight. If the ice has mass \(m\), the displaced water has mass \(m\), so its volume is:

\[
V_{\text{displaced}}=\frac{m}{\rho_{\text{water}}}
\]

When the ice melts, it becomes water with the same mass \(m\). The meltwater volume is likewise:

\[
V_{\text{melted}}=\frac{m}{\rho_{\text{water}}}
\]

Thus, the water produced by melting occupies exactly the volume that had already been displaced by the floating ice. The water level therefore stays essentially unchanged, so a glass that was not overflowing beforehand will not overflow merely because the floating ice melts.

This assumes the ice is ordinary water ice, it is freely floating, and the glass contains only water and ice. Small real-world effects—such as temperature changes, dissolved air, impurities, or surface-tension effects—can cause tiny deviations, but they are normally negligible.
