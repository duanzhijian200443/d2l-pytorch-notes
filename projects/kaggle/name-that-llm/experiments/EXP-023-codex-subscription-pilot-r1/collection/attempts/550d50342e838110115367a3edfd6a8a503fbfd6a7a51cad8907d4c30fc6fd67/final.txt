Oral history preserves experiences that official records often overlook by recording people’s memories in their own voices. Government files, newspapers, and institutional archives may emphasize leaders, policies, and formal events, while excluding everyday life and the perspectives of marginalized communities. Interviews can capture emotions, family traditions, workplace culture, discrimination, migration, resistance, and other details rarely documented in writing. They also reveal how individuals understood events, not merely what happened, helping researchers explore identity, memory, and the long-term effects of historical change. In this way, oral testimony can challenge dominant narratives and fill gaps in the archive.

However, researchers must remember that memory is selective and can change over time. Interviewees may forget details, merge separate events, or interpret the past through later experiences. The interviewer’s questions and the interview setting can also shape the account. Oral histories should therefore be treated as situated interpretations rather than exact transcripts of the past and, when possible, compared with other testimony and documentary evidence.
