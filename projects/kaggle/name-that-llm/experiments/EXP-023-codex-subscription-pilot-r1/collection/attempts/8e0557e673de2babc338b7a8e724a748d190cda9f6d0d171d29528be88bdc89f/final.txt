The team should treat the enthusiasm as a promising signal, not proof of product value. Comments capture initial appeal, social politeness, or excitement about novelty; repeat usage is stronger evidence that the feature solves a recurring problem. The gap suggests the feature may be interesting but not useful enough in an ongoing workflow, may be difficult to discover or use, or may address a low-frequency need.

The team should examine behavior by cohort and use case: who tried it, how often, where they dropped off, and whether repeat users share particular characteristics. They should also interview both repeat and one-time users, asking what they expected, what prevented a second use, and what alternative they chose. Then test focused improvements—such as onboarding, integration, speed, or a clearer recurring use case—and measure activation, repeat usage, retention, and downstream outcomes.

In short, celebrate the positive feedback, but prioritize observed repeated behavior and validate whether the feature creates durable value rather than temporary excitement.
