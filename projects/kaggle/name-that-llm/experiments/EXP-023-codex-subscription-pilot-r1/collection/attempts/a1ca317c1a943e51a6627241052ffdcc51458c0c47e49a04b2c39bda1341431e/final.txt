The path taken by water in a river is the large-scale route determined by the river channel—from its source toward its mouth. It is shaped by the landscape, gravity, erosion, rocks, bends, and obstacles. This path describes the overall direction of the flowing water, much like a road describes the route vehicles can take.

The movement of an individual water molecule along that path is more complicated. Although the molecule is generally carried downstream by the river’s current, it does not travel in a perfectly straight line or at a constant speed. It may move in small swirling motions, rise or sink, collide with other molecules, and be temporarily trapped near the riverbed or banks. In turbulent water, its motion includes random, irregular fluctuations superimposed on the overall downstream flow.

Thus, the river’s path represents the average or organized motion of the water as a whole, while an individual molecule follows a constantly changing trajectory within that flow. Over time, the molecule may move downstream, but its exact route can differ greatly from the river’s central path. This distinction is similar to the difference between the direction of traffic on a road and the detailed, sometimes erratic path followed by one particular car.
