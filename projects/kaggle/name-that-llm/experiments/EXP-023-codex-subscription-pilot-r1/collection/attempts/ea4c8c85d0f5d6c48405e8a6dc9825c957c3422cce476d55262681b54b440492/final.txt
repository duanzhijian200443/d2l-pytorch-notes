Mara returned from the marsh with a memory card full of victories: a heron rising blue-gray from reeds, dragonflies balanced on cattails, sunset spilling copper across the water. At home, she edited until midnight, pleased with every frame.

Then she found the blur.

It was only a smear of gold and green, tilted wildly, with a dark shape near the center. Mara remembered the moment: she had heard wings behind her, spun too quickly, and pressed the shutter as a fox leapt across the path. Her camera had focused on nothing.

She almost deleted it.

Instead, she kept it.

Months later, visitors praised her sharp heron and brilliant sunset. Mara thanked them, but her favorite photograph remained the useless one. It held the surprise she had felt—the thump of paws, the rush of wings, the sudden certainty that the marsh was alive beyond her ability to capture it.

The blur reminded her that photography was not possession. Sometimes it was simply proof that she had been startled into paying attention.
