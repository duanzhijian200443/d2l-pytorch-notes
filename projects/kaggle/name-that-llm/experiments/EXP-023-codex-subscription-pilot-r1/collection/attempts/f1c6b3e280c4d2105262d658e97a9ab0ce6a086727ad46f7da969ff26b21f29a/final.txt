Dividing a project by task count does not necessarily create an equal division of work because tasks can differ greatly in complexity, time, risk, and required expertise. For example, one team might complete ten simple tasks, such as formatting documents, configuring routine settings, or fixing minor issues. The other team might complete only five tasks, but those tasks could involve designing a system, solving difficult technical problems, coordinating dependencies, or making decisions with significant consequences. The second team may therefore spend far more time and mental effort despite having fewer tasks.

A fair division should consider the estimated effort and difficulty of each task rather than simply counting items. Useful measures may include person-hours, complexity points, risk, uncertainty, and the specialized skills required. Teams should also account for hidden work, such as testing, documentation, communication, debugging, and rework. Task count can be a convenient starting point, but it is an unreliable measure of workload unless the tasks are genuinely similar in size and complexity. Equal numbers of tasks do not automatically mean equal contributions.
