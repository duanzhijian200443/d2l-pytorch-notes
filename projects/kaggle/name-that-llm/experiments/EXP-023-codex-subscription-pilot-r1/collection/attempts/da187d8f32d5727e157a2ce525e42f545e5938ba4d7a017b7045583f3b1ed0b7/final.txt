The song came through the grocery store speakers too slowly, each note stretched and heavy, as if it were being pulled through honey.

Mara stopped beside the oranges.

She knew it anyway.

It was “Bluebird,” the song her father used to play on Sunday mornings, before the apartment filled with coffee steam and arguments about bills. At the wrong speed, the singer sounded older, almost tired. The melody dragged its feet, revealing spaces Mara had never noticed between the notes.

Then she remembered Mr. Alvarez from upstairs.

Years ago, when her father had been in the hospital and Mara was pretending everything was fine, Mr. Alvarez had knocked on their door with a paper bag of warm empanadas. He hadn’t asked questions. He had simply left the bag on the table, repaired the loose hinge on their kitchen cabinet, and told her, “Eat before it gets cold.”

Mara had thanked him, but only because politeness required it. She had forgotten the tenderness of the gesture until now.

The distorted song continued above the humming refrigerators.

She carried the oranges to the register and paused, then turned toward the bakery. Mr. Alvarez still lived upstairs, she thought. Maybe he would remember her.

She bought two pastries, walked home, and climbed the stairs slowly.

At his door, she raised her hand.

The song was still playing in her head—wrong, aching, and somehow exactly right.
