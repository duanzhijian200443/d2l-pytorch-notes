The laundromat radio was dying slowly. Its batteries dragged a bright pop song into something mournful, the singer’s voice deepening between the dryers.

Mara stopped folding a towel.

At first she knew only the shape of it: three rising notes, a pause, then the chorus arriving late. She tapped the plastic table, faster than the music.

“Oh,” she said.

Her mother used to sing it while driving, always too fast. One hand on the wheel, the other searching her handbag for peppermints. Mara, fourteen, would turn toward the window and pretend not to hear.

She remembered the winter concert. Her mother had arrived in her supermarket uniform, and Mara had hurried past her afterward, ashamed of the green polyester shirt, the name badge, the smell of oranges.

But there had been something in her shoes.

Mara laid the towel down.

New insoles. The thick, soft kind. She’d complained once that the concert shoes hurt, then forgotten. Before leaving for her shift, her mother must have cut the insoles to fit and slipped them beneath the buckles.

Mara had stood comfortably through the whole performance.

The radio faltered. Someone slapped its side.

She reached for her phone, then checked the time. Her mother would still be awake.
