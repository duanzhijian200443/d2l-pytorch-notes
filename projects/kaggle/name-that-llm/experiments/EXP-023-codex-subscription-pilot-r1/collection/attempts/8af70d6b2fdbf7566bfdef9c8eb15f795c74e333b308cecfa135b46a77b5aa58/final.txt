A floating piece of ice does not usually make a glass overflow when it melts because of **Archimedes’ principle**.

While floating, the ice displaces a volume of water whose weight is exactly equal to the weight of the ice. Ice is less dense than liquid water, so part of it sticks above the surface, but the submerged portion still displaces enough water to support the ice’s entire weight.

When the ice melts, its mass does not change. The resulting liquid water therefore has the same weight as the original ice. Because the floating ice had already displaced precisely that weight of water, the melted water occupies exactly the volume that was previously displaced by the submerged part of the ice. Thus, the water level remains essentially unchanged.

For example, if an ice cube weighs 10 grams, it displaces 10 grams—or about 10 millilitres—of liquid water while floating. After melting, it becomes approximately 10 millilitres of water, filling the same displaced volume.

This result assumes the glass contains only ordinary water and floating ice. Dissolved substances, trapped air, objects embedded in the ice, or unusually precise temperature-related density changes can produce small differences, but under normal conditions melting floating ice does not cause overflow.
