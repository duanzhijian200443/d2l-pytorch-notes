The laundromat was nearly empty when the dryer began to squeal.

Three short notes, a pause, then two more—stretched thin by the slow turn of the drum. Mara looked up from her phone. The rhythm was wrong, dragging like a record played at half speed, but beneath the groan of belts and tumbling buttons, she knew it.

“Blue Moon.”

Her father used to whistle it while washing dishes, always too fast, as if the song were late for something. She hadn’t thought of that in years. What she remembered instead were slammed doors, missed birthdays, his silence at her mother’s funeral.

The dryer turned again.

Blue... moon...

And suddenly she saw a winter morning she had misplaced: herself at sixteen, furious and shivering at a bus stop after he’d forbidden her from taking the car. Snow had soaked through her canvas shoes. She had found, inside her backpack, two hand warmers, a thermos of coffee made mostly of milk, and a folded twenty-dollar bill. No note.

At the time, she’d assumed her mother had packed them.

But her mother hated coffee. Her father carried hand warmers in every coat.

The dryer clicked off. In the quiet, Mara opened their old message thread. Her thumb hovered above the blank field.

Then she typed, *Do you still whistle Blue Moon?*
