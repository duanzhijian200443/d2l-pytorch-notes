Asking users to confirm a destructive action twice can be helpful when the consequences are serious, irreversible, or difficult to understand at the moment of action. A second step creates a pause, gives users another opportunity to notice what will be deleted, and can prevent accidental clicks caused by misalignment, haste, or misleading interface elements. It is especially appropriate for actions such as permanently deleting an account, removing essential data, or sending an irreversible transaction. The second confirmation should ideally restate the consequence clearly, rather than simply repeat “Are you sure?”

However, repeated confirmation quickly becomes ineffective when it is used too often or without meaningful new information. Users learn to treat confirmation dialogs as routine obstacles and click through them automatically—a behavior often called confirmation fatigue. This is particularly likely when the first warning already explains the risk, the second prompt uses vague language, or similar prompts appear for minor actions. Excessive repetition also slows workflows, frustrates users, and may encourage them to disable safeguards mentally or mechanically.

A good design matches friction to risk. Use one clear confirmation for ordinary destructive actions, and reserve a second, more deliberate step for exceptionally harmful or irreversible ones. Where appropriate, replace generic prompts with specific actions, such as typing the item’s name, selecting a recovery period, or offering an undo option.
