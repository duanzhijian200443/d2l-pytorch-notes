The plant is showing **phototropism**—directional growth in response to light. In most young shoots, this means bending toward the light source, such as a window. This helps the plant position its leaves to capture more light for photosynthesis.

Light-sensitive proteins called **phototropins**, especially responsive to blue light, detect the uneven illumination. Their activation changes how the plant hormone **auxin** is distributed in the growing shoot, so more auxin accumulates on the shaded side.

In shoots, auxin promotes cell elongation. It triggers processes that loosen cell walls, allowing cells to expand as they take up water. Because cells on the shaded side elongate more than those on the illuminated side, the two sides grow at different rates. The longer shaded side pushes the shoot into a curve toward the window—rather like a strip that bends when one side becomes longer than the other.

The bending takes time because it is a **growth response**, not an immediate movement like a muscle contraction. Light detection and chemical signalling must be followed by hormone redistribution, cell-wall changes, and enough unequal cell expansion to produce a visible curve. Depending on the plant and conditions, noticeable bending can develop over hours or days, mainly in young, actively growing regions.
