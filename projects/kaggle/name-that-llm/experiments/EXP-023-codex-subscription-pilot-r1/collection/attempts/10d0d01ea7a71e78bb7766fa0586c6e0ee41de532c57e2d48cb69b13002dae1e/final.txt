Try a **“ten-minute minimum, one-zone only”** rule.

Before starting, define a target that is deliberately smaller than “clean the room”: clear one chair, wash five dishes, sort one pile, or wipe one countertop. Then set a ten-minute timer and give yourself explicit permission to stop when it rings—even if the room remains imperfect.

Use this script:

> “I am not cleaning the whole room. I am improving this one area for ten minutes.”

During those ten minutes, avoid expanding the job. If you notice another task, write it down rather than switching to it. When the timer ends, pause and choose consciously: stop without guilt, or do another ten-minute round. Continuing is optional, not the hidden requirement.

It may also help to define three levels of completion:

- **Good enough:** one small area is visibly better.  
- **Better:** the most obvious clutter is handled.  
- **Perfect:** everything is fully cleaned and organized.

For everyday tasks, aim only for “good enough.” This retrains your brain to associate starting with a limited, manageable commitment rather than an exhausting standard.

The key is to measure success by **starting and honoring the boundary**, not by finishing the room. A partly improved space is useful; an imagined perfect room that prevents action is not.
