Every evening, Mara practiced beneath the hall’s unlit chandelier. Without an audience, the room seemed larger, its rows of velvet seats stretching beyond the reach of her small rehearsal lamp.

She was preparing a Bach suite. For three weeks, the same passage had defeated her. Her bow hurried; her fingers tightened. Each mistake traveled to the balcony and returned.

On Thursday, she stopped halfway through.

“Again,” someone said.

A cleaner stood beside the rear doors, one hand resting on a mop.

Mara lowered her cello. “How long have you been there?”

“Tonight?”

She smiled despite herself. “Any night.”

“Since you started leaving the side door open.”

He looked embarrassed, as though listening were something he should have paid for.

“I can work somewhere else.”

“No.” Mara straightened her music. “Does it sound better?”

He considered this seriously.

“The beginning does. That bit you keep stopping at—I don’t know what comes after.”

For the first time all week, she laughed.

She began again. At the difficult passage, her finger slipped. She nearly stopped, then saw him waiting by the doors.

She played on.

When the last note faded, he remained still for a moment before lifting his mop.

“Good,” he said. “Now I’ve heard it.”

The next evening, Mara opened the side door before unpacking her cello.
