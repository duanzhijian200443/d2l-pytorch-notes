Asking users to confirm a destructive action twice can help when the consequences are severe, difficult to reverse, or easy to misunderstand. Deleting an account, wiping a database, or permanently removing shared files may justify an additional checkpoint. The second step is especially useful when it adds information or requires meaningful attention—for example, showing how many records will disappear, explaining who else will be affected, or asking the user to type the resource’s name. This can catch accidental clicks and mistaken assumptions about the action’s scope.

Repeated confirmation becomes ineffective when both prompts simply ask “Are you sure?” Users quickly learn to dismiss repetitive dialogs automatically, particularly when they encounter them frequently. This habituation turns a safety measure into an obstacle rather than a moment of reflection. Excessive warnings can also reduce attention to genuinely important alerts, while adding frustration and accessibility burdens.

A better approach is to match friction to risk. Use clear, specific wording and explicit action labels such as “Permanently delete account,” rather than “OK.” Where possible, offer undo, a recovery period, backups, or a preview of the consequences. Reserve double confirmation for exceptional risks, and ensure each step serves a distinct purpose. Two clicks are not inherently safer than one informed decision.
