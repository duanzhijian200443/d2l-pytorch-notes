Yes—if the replicas are clearly labeled and the museum manages the activity thoughtfully. Touching sturdy objects makes exhibits more accessible, especially for children, blind or low-vision visitors, and people who learn best through physical interaction. It can turn passive viewing into memorable discovery and help visitors understand weight, texture, craftsmanship, and use in ways labels cannot.

The cost is a noisier, busier gallery, which may reduce reflection and disturb visitors seeking a quiet experience. Replicas also require cleaning, replacement, staff attention, and clear rules to prevent confusion with originals. A small museum can balance these concerns by placing hands-on objects in a designated area, using durable materials, limiting numbers when needed, and offering quieter hours. The educational and inclusion benefits usually justify the trade-off.
