Standardizing units of measurement made trade between communities easier by giving buyers and sellers a shared way to compare quantities. A merchant could purchase a kilogram of grain or a metre of cloth without first translating unfamiliar local measures. This reduced misunderstandings, limited opportunities for fraud, simplified contracts, and helped markets expand. Governments could also collect taxes and regulate commerce more consistently.

However, disappearing local measures could mean losing more than old terminology. Such measures often reflected regional livelihoods, environmental conditions, and practical knowledge—for example, land measured by how much seed it required or how long it took to plough. Replacing them could weaken cultural identity and local autonomy, while disadvantaging people unfamiliar with the new system.
