A word-for-word translation can preserve the visible images in a proverb—animals, weather, tools, food, or actions—while losing what the proverb is actually used to *do* in conversation. Proverbs are compressed cultural knowledge. Their literal picture is often only a vehicle for a shared lesson, warning, joke, or social judgment.

For example, speakers may hear a familiar image and immediately understand “be patient,” “do not trust appearances,” or “small efforts add up.” A reader from another culture may understand every translated word but not recognize that conventional implication. The image may also have different associations: an owl, dog, color, or type of weather can suggest very different qualities across cultures.

Practical meaning depends on context as well. A proverb might be used to comfort someone, criticize them indirectly, excuse an outcome, or advise caution. Literal translation does not automatically carry its tone, level of politeness, or appropriate situation of use.

Therefore, good proverb translation often needs more than lexical accuracy. It may use an equivalent proverb in the target language, add a brief explanation, or translate the intended lesson rather than the exact imagery. The best choice depends on whether preserving cultural flavor or conveying the speaker’s practical message is more important.
