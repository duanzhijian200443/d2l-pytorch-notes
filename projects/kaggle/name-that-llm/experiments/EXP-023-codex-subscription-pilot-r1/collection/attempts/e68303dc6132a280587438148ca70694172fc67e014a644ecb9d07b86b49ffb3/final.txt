Mara hated waiting. She drummed through red lights, abandoned microwaves at thirty seconds, and considered any queue longer than three people a personal insult.

But tonight she sat quietly on the back steps, wrapped in her father’s old wool coat, watching the dark garden.

There was no schedule for the fox.

Her neighbor had seen it once near the bins. A rust-red blur, thin as hunger. Mara had left water and scraps beneath the pear tree, then carried out a chair. Midnight passed. Frost silvered the grass. Her phone died unnoticed in her pocket.

She listened instead: leaves ticking along the wall, a distant train, her own breath slowing.

At two, something moved behind the shed.

Mara did not lean forward. She did not reach for the camera beside her. She scarcely blinked.

The fox stepped into the moonlight, cautious and bright-eyed, and lowered its head to drink.

For once, Mara felt no triumph at being rewarded.

Only gratitude that she had been there when the world chose to arrive.
