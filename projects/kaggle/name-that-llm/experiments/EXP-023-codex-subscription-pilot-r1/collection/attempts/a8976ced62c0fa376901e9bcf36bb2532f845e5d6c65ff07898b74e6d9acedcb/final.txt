A sunlit sidewalk patch can become much hotter than the surrounding air because concrete absorbs solar energy and stores heat. Its temperature also depends on its color, material, moisture, slope, and how long it has been exposed to sunlight. A thermometer touching or positioned very close to that surface may therefore measure the sidewalk’s heat rather than the neighborhood’s air temperature.

Air temperature varies across a neighborhood as well. Shade, trees, buildings, traffic, wind, lawns, and bodies of water create different local conditions. For a representative reading, meteorologists measure air in a shaded, ventilated location, usually over natural ground and at a standard height. Multiple properly placed measurements are even better. One sunlit sidewalk reading describes a specific hot surface and its immediate surroundings—not the entire neighborhood.
