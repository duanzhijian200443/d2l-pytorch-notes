Clouds act like a thermal blanket at night. The ground continuously emits infrared radiation toward space, allowing its surface to cool. Under clear skies, much of this radiation escapes directly to space, so the ground and nearby air lose heat rapidly. Clouds, however, absorb and re-emit infrared radiation downward, returning some of that energy to the surface and reducing net heat loss. They also mix with the atmosphere in ways that help limit near-surface cooling. Consequently, clear nights often produce stronger radiational cooling, colder ground temperatures, and sometimes frost or temperature inversions. The effect is greatest when winds are light and the air is dry, because turbulent mixing and water vapor are then less able to slow the surface’s radiative heat loss.
