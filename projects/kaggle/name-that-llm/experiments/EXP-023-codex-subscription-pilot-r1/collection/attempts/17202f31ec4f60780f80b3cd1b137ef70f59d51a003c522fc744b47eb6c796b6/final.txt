Familiar rituals often outlive their original purpose because they acquire new functions. A practice that began as a practical solution, religious observance, or safety measure may later become a symbol of belonging, continuity, or respect. People repeat it not because they remember why it started, but because “this is how we do things.”

Rituals are also emotionally efficient. In uncertain moments—holidays, grief, weddings, meals, departures—they provide a script. The repetition reduces decision-making and creates comfort through predictability. Shared rituals can signal trust and group membership: participating shows that one understands the group’s norms.

Over time, the origin story may disappear while the action remains easy to teach and imitate. Children learn the gesture before learning any explanation, if an explanation still exists. Sometimes people then create new meanings for an old custom, adapting it to current values. In that sense, forgetting the original reason does not make a ritual meaningless; its meaning may simply have shifted from utility to connection, memory, and identity.
