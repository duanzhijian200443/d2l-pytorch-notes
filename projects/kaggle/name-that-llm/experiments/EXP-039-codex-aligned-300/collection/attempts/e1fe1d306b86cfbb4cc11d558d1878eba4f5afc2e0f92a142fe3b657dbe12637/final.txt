Explaining a historical person’s choices means examining the beliefs, pressures, institutions, and available alternatives that shaped their decisions. It asks why an action seemed reasonable, necessary, or advantageous to that person, without assuming it was morally right. Excusing every consequence goes further: it treats context as a blanket defense, minimizing suffering or suggesting that responsibility disappears because an injustice was common. A careful historical judgment avoids both easy condemnation and automatic absolution. It considers what people could realistically have known, whether contemporaries challenged their conduct, and how much freedom they had to act differently. Context can qualify blame and clarify motives, but it does not erase victims’ experiences. Understanding a decision and judging its consequences are complementary tasks, not opposing ones.
