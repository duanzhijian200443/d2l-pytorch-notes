Oral history preserves experiences that official records often overlook by recording people’s memories, interpretations, and accounts of everyday life. Government documents, institutional archives, and newspapers tend to emphasize powerful individuals, formal decisions, and public events, leaving less room for the perspectives of marginalized communities, workers, migrants, or families. Interviews can reveal how people experienced discrimination, adapted to economic change, maintained cultural traditions, or understood major historical events in their own lives. They also capture emotion, language, and personal meaning that administrative records rarely convey. Preserving these testimonies allows future generations to encounter a wider range of voices and helps researchers question or enrich established historical narratives. However, memory is selective and can change over time: interviewees may unintentionally misremember dates, reinterpret experiences through later beliefs, or shape their accounts in response to an interviewer. Researchers should therefore assess testimonies critically, consider the interview context, and compare them with other evidence where possible, while recognizing that subjective recollections themselves offer valuable historical insight.
