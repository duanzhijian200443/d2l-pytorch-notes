Tides arise because the Moon’s gravity pulls on different parts of Earth with slightly different strength and direction, rather than pulling the whole planet and ocean uniformly toward it. The Moon attracts the near side of Earth more strongly than it attracts Earth’s center, producing a bulge of ocean water toward the Moon. It attracts the far side less strongly than it attracts Earth’s center, so there is also a relative bulge on the far side. Earth and Moon orbit their common center of mass, and the motion of that orbit helps create this far-side effect; it is not simply water being flung away in a separate force. The solid Earth is affected too, although less visibly, while the mobile ocean adjusts around it. As Earth rotates beneath these two broad tidal bulges, most coastal locations pass through two high tides and two low tides during a lunar day. Water does not remain collected on the Moon-facing side because Earth’s gravity holds the oceans to the planet, the Moon’s pull is only a small difference across Earth’s diameter, and the rotating Earth continually carries shorelines into and out of the bulges. Continents, seafloor shape, coastline geometry, and resonance in ocean basins modify the timing and height of local tides, while the Sun also strengthens or weakens them.
