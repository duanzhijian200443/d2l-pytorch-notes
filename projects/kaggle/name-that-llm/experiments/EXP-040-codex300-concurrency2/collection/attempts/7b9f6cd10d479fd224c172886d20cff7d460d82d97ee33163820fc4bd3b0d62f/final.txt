A historian may use several incomplete sources because no single account is likely to capture the whole past accurately. Every source reflects its creator’s knowledge, purpose, social position, and biases. An official report may record dates and decisions but ignore ordinary people’s experiences, while a diary may reveal personal feelings yet misunderstand wider events. By comparing letters, newspapers, government records, photographs, artifacts, and oral testimony, historians can identify agreements, contradictions, omissions, and exaggerations. Independent sources may corroborate a claim, while conflicting evidence can show that people experienced or interpreted the same event differently. Combining partial perspectives therefore produces a fuller, more nuanced reconstruction. Searching for one “perfect” account risks treating a limited viewpoint as complete and overlooking the uncertainty, complexity, and contested meanings that are central to historical inquiry.
