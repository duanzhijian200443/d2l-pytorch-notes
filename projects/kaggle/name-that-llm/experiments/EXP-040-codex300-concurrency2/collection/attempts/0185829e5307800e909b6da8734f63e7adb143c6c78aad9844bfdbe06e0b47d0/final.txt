Enthusiastic comments are encouraging, but they are evidence of initial appeal rather than sustained value. The team should treat the gap between praise and repeat use as a signal to investigate, not as proof that the feature has succeeded or failed. People may like the idea, enjoy a novelty moment, want to be supportive, or encounter friction that comments do not reveal. Measure return rates by relevant user segment, time to first value, completion, frequency of the underlying need, and where people abandon the flow. Compare repeat behavior with a clear baseline and with the feature’s intended job: a rarely needed tool may have low frequency but still deliver value, while a habitual feature should retain users. Follow up with both repeat users and one-time users to learn what changed after the first experience. Then form testable hypotheses—such as unclear value, poor onboarding, missing integrations, or weak recurring triggers—and run focused experiments. Behavioral evidence should outweigh compliments when prioritizing investment.
