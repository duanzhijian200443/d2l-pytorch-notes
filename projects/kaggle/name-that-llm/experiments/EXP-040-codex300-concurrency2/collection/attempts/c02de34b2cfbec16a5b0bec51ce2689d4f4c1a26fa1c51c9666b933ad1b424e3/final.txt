Asking users to confirm a destructive action twice can help when the consequences are severe, difficult to reverse, or easy to misunderstand. Deleting an account, permanently erasing shared files, or removing a production database may justify an additional checkpoint, especially if the first confirmation could be triggered accidentally. The second prompt is most useful when it adds meaningful information: exactly what will disappear, who else will be affected, and whether recovery is possible. For particularly consequential actions, asking users to type the resource’s name can encourage deliberate attention rather than another automatic click. However, repeated confirmation becomes ineffective when both prompts say essentially the same thing or appear for routine, low-risk actions. Users learn to dismiss them reflexively, creating confirmation fatigue and weakening the protection even when a warning genuinely matters. Extra friction can also frustrate experienced users without improving their understanding. Good design therefore prioritizes the quality and timing of confirmation over the number of dialogs. Clear button labels, a summary of consequences, and a visually distinct destructive option can make one confirmation more effective than two generic warnings. Where feasible, undo, soft deletion, backups, or a recovery period provide stronger protection by allowing mistakes to be corrected rather than merely asking users to promise they will not make them.
