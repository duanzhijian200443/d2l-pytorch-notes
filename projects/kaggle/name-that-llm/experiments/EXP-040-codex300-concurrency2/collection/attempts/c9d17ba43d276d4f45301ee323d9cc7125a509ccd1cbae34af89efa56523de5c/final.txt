A search system can return a relevant document even when it lacks the exact words typed because it often searches for meaning and related forms rather than performing only literal word matching. It may normalize terms by lowercasing, removing punctuation, correcting spelling, or reducing words to stems, so a query for “running” can match “run” or “ran.” Synonym dictionaries and language models can associate related expressions, allowing “automobile” to retrieve documents about “cars.” Modern semantic search represents queries and documents as numerical vectors that capture contextual meaning; consequently, a page discussing treatments for high blood pressure may be considered relevant to a query about hypertension without using that precise term. Search engines can also expand a query using historical search behavior, common abbreviations, translations, or terms frequently appearing in similar documents. Ranking signals further help select useful results: a document may have a relevant title, linked anchor text, metadata, or strong topical similarity even if its body omits the query phrase. These techniques improve recall and make search more tolerant of vocabulary differences, although systems must balance them carefully to avoid returning results that are broadly related but not actually useful.
