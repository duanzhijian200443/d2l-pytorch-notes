The claim that a good explanation should always avoid technical vocabulary is too absolute. Technical terms can make an explanation more precise, efficient, and accurate, especially when the audience already understands the relevant field. For example, a doctor explaining “hypertension” to another doctor need not replace it with a lengthy phrase such as “persistently elevated blood pressure.” Similarly, students cannot fully learn a subject if every specialized concept is removed from the explanation. However, technical vocabulary becomes a problem when it is used without considering the listener’s knowledge. Unfamiliar terms can confuse, exclude, or create the illusion of understanding, particularly when simpler language would communicate the idea just as well. A good explanation therefore manages technical language rather than banning it. The speaker should assess the audience, introduce essential terms clearly, define them in plain language, and use examples or comparisons when helpful. It is also important to distinguish necessary terminology from jargon used merely to sound authoritative. In some situations, avoiding a technical word may make an explanation less accurate; in others, using one without explanation may make it inaccessible. The best principle is not “avoid technical vocabulary,” but “use the simplest language that preserves the needed meaning, and explain specialized terms when the audience may not know them.”
