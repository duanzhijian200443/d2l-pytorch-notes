Ocean tides occur because the Moon’s gravity pulls unevenly on Earth: it is strongest on the side facing the Moon, weaker at Earth’s center, and weakest on the far side. This difference, called the tidal force, stretches the oceans into two broad bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side. The far-side bulge is not caused by the Moon directly pulling water outward; rather, the Earth and Moon orbit their common center of mass, creating inertia that makes water tend to move away from Earth’s center on that side. Earth’s rotation carries coastlines through these bulges, producing regularly changing high and low tides instead of allowing water to remain permanently piled up. In reality, the ocean does not form two perfect global humps. Continents, underwater ridges, varying depths, friction, and the shape of ocean basins redirect and slow the water, causing tides to behave like enormous waves that rotate around ocean basins. Local tides may therefore occur once or twice daily and can differ greatly in height and timing. The Sun also contributes tidal forces, strengthening or weakening the Moon’s effect depending on their alignment. Thus, tides result from a continuously changing balance among differential gravity, inertia, Earth’s rotation, and the constrained movement of water around the planet.
