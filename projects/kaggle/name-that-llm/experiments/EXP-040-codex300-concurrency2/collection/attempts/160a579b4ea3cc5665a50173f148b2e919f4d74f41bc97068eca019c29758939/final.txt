Ocean tides arise because the Moon’s gravity pulls unevenly across Earth, not because it simply draws all seawater toward itself. The Moon pulls more strongly on Earth’s near side than on its center, and more weakly on the far side. Earth and its oceans are falling together through space under lunar gravity, so these differences in acceleration stretch the oceans along the Earth–Moon direction. In an idealized picture, this produces two tidal bulges: one facing the Moon and one facing away. On the far side, Earth’s center accelerates toward the Moon more strongly than the water does, leaving that water farther outward relative to the center. Water does not accumulate indefinitely because Earth’s own gravity and the pressure differences created by changing sea levels oppose further redistribution. More importantly, Earth rotates relative to the Moon, so the lunar pull’s direction continually changes for any particular coastline. Most places therefore pass through roughly two high tides and two low tides each lunar day, about 24 hours and 50 minutes. Real oceans cannot instantly assume the idealized shape: continents, depth, friction, and basin resonances turn the response into complex moving waves. The Sun’s gravity also contributes, strengthening or weakening tides depending on its alignment with the Moon.
