A light source with a finite size, such as a lamp or the Sun, emits light from many slightly different positions. Each position forms its own shadow of the object, and these overlapping shadows create a region of partial darkness called the penumbra. Close to the object, the individual shadows still overlap strongly, so the boundary appears relatively sharp and a darker umbra may remain. As the shadow travels farther away, the separate shadows spread apart because of the light source’s angular size. Their overlap becomes less complete, producing a wider penumbra and a fuzzier edge. A larger light source, or one closer to the object, has a greater angular size and therefore creates a broader, softer shadow boundary. A small or distant source produces a sharper shadow.
