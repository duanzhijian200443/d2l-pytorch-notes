A search system may return a relevant document even when it does not contain the exact words typed because modern search engines try to understand meaning, not merely match character strings. They may use stemming or lemmatization to connect different forms of a word, such as “running,” “runs,” and “run,” and synonym expansion to associate terms like “car” with “automobile.” They can also interpret context and intent: a query for “how to fix a leaking faucet” may retrieve a document about “repairing a dripping tap” because both phrases describe the same problem. Semantic search systems represent queries and documents as mathematical vectors based on their meanings, allowing conceptually similar text to be matched even when its vocabulary differs. Search engines may also use knowledge graphs, spelling correction, translations, user location, and patterns learned from previous searches to infer what the user likely wants. In addition, links, document titles, metadata, anchor text, and surrounding passages can signal relevance even if the precise query terms are absent from the main text. Ranking algorithms combine these signals with factors such as authority, freshness, and usefulness. As a result, retrieval is often based on the relationship between ideas and the probable purpose of the query rather than on exact word overlap alone.
