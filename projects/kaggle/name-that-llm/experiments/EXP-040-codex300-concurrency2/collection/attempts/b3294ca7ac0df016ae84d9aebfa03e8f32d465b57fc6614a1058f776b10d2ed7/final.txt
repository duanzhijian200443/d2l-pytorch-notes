Collecting objects as souvenirs and collecting them as historical evidence differ mainly in purpose, selection, and interpretation. A souvenir is usually kept because it carries personal meaning: it recalls a journey, a special occasion, a place, or a relationship. Its value lies primarily in the memories and emotions attached to it, rather than in what it can establish about the past. A mass-produced postcard, for example, may be precious because it reminds someone of a family holiday. By contrast, an object collected as historical evidence is preserved to help answer questions about past lives, events, and societies. Its significance depends on its context, including who made or used it, when and where it originated, and how it relates to other sources. Collectors and historians therefore need to document its provenance and assess its authenticity, limitations, and representativeness. The same postcard could provide evidence about tourism, transportation, popular imagery, or social attitudes. These categories are not mutually exclusive: a personal souvenir can later become valuable historical evidence, while an artifact in an archive may also hold emotional meaning. Ultimately, the distinction rests less on the object itself than on why it is preserved, what information accompanies it, and how people use it.
