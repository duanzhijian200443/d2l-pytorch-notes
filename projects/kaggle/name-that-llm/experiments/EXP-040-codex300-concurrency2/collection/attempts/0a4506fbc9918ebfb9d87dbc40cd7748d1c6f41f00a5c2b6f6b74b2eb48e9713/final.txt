At seven forty, the orchestra had every chair except one, and the conductor refused to begin. From the lobby came the impatient rustle of programs. “There are folding chairs downstairs,” the manager said. “Not that chair,” said Mara, the second violinist. The missing chair was wooden, ugly, and shorter than the others. A stagehand had moved it while clearing space for the piano. They searched dressing rooms, then the loading dock. Finally, a cleaner found it beside the freight elevator, holding a bucket. Mara wiped the seat and carried it onto the stage herself. The manager watched her father settle into it, his cello between his knees. “He can manage another seat for one night, surely?” he whispered. “His feet need to reach the floor,” she said. “Since the stroke, he can’t balance on the higher ones.” Her father tested his bow, then looked toward the impatient hall. “Well,” he said, “what are we waiting for?”
