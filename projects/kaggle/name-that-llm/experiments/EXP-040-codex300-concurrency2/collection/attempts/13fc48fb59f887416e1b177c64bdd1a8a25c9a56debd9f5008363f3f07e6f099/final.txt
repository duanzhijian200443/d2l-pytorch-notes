Mara returned from the coast with a memory card full of triumphs: gulls suspended above silver water, fishing boats glowing in the late sun, and a lighthouse framed perfectly between two black cliffs. She was only an amateur, but every photograph seemed sharp enough for a magazine. Every photograph except one. Near the end of the outing, as rain began to stipple the harbor, she had turned suddenly and pressed the shutter without checking her focus. The result was a smear of blue coats, wet pavement, and amber light. Her friends urged her to delete it. Instead, Mara enlarged it on her screen. At the center, barely recognizable, stood her father, laughing as he tried to hold an umbrella against the wind. He had spent the day carrying her spare lenses, waiting patiently while she adjusted settings, and pretending not to be cold. The blur made his laughter seem larger than his face, as if joy itself had shaken the camera. Months later, after illness made outings impossible for him, Mara printed the lighthouse, the gulls, and the boats for an exhibition. Yet the picture she kept beside her bed was the ruined one. The others showed exactly what the coast had looked like. Only that blurred photograph showed how the day had felt.
