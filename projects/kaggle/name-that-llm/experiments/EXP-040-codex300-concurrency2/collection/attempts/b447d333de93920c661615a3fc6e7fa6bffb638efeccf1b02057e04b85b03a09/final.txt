Each evening, after the last chairs had been stacked and the stage lights dimmed to a single amber pool, Mara returned to the empty concert hall with her violin. She practiced difficult passages until her fingers ached, letting mistakes rise boldly into the red velvet silence. For weeks, she believed the hall belonged only to her and the dust motes drifting above the orchestra pit. Then, one rainy night, she finished a hesitant rendition of a sonata and heard applause: three soft, careful claps from the rear aisle. Startled, she found Mr. Alvarez, the cleaner, standing beside his cart with a mop in one hand. He apologized at once, saying he had tried not to interrupt. “I listen while I work,” he admitted. “You make the place feel alive.” Mara learned that he came early every evening, polishing rails and sweeping balconies while she played below. He had once wanted to study piano, but had left school to help his family. The next night, Mara brought two chairs to the stage. Before practicing, she asked which piece he liked best. Mr. Alvarez smiled shyly and named an old waltz. She played it slowly, not for empty seats, but for the one listener who had taught her that music was never truly alone.
