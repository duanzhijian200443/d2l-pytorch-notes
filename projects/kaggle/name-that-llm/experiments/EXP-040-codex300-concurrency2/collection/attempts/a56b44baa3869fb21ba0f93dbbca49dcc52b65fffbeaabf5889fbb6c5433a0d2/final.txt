By sunset, Nora had taken ninety-three photographs of the harbor, and ninety-two were the best pictures she had ever made. Gulls hung sharp against the clouds. Fishing boats carried perfect reflections. Even the old man selling oranges had turned toward her just as sunlight found his face. At home, she spread the pictures across her computer screen, delighted by how much she had learned since buying her secondhand camera. Then she reached the last image. It was hopeless: a pale streak, a crooked horizon, something red dissolving into blue. Her finger hovered over Delete. She remembered taking it. Her father had come along that morning, insisting his knees could manage the pier. While she adjusted her shutter speed, he had caught her sleeve and pointed at a seal surfacing between the boats. She had swung the camera too quickly. The seal was gone before she could try again. “Did you get him?” he had asked. “Not really.” “You should’ve seen his face.” He had made an absurd, whiskered expression, and she had laughed so hard she needed to sit down. Nora deleted nothing. She printed the blurred picture and put it beside her desk. Months later, her father noticed it. “That’s a terrible photograph,” he said, and made the face again.
