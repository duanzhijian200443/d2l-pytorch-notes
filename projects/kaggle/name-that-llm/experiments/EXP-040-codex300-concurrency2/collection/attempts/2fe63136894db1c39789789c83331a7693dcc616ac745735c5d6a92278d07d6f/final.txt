Oral history preserves experiences that official records often omit because it invites people to describe daily life, emotions, relationships, informal work, community practices, and personal responses to major events in their own words. Government files, newspapers, institutional reports, and business documents tend to reflect the priorities of those with authority, while interviews can recover perspectives from marginalized groups, workers, migrants, families, and witnesses whose views were never formally documented. A survivor’s account may reveal how a policy affected household routines, how neighbors supported one another during a crisis, or why individuals made choices that records merely list. Spoken testimony can also preserve local language, memory, and cultural meaning that written sources flatten or ignore. Researchers should remember, however, that memory is selective and shaped by later experiences, emotions, and the interview setting. Oral accounts should therefore be interpreted critically and, where possible, compared with other evidence rather than treated as perfectly objective factual records.
