At seven thirty, the audience began clapping impatiently while stagehands searched for the orchestra’s missing chair. It looked ordinary: black wood, scratched legs, a strip of blue tape beneath the seat. The conductor refused to begin without it, though no musician seemed short of a place to sit. Mara, the youngest usher, finally remembered seeing a delivery driver carry a chair toward the loading dock. She found it wedged beneath a door handle, propping open the fire exit. When she pulled it free, an alarm shrieked, and a thin smell of gas drifted from the alley. Firefighters discovered a leaking cylinder in the caterer’s van, parked beside the building’s air intake. The blue-taped chair was not valuable, nor lucky, nor reserved for a famous soloist. It was simply the chair assigned to the percussionist stationed nearest that exit, required by the hall’s evacuation plan. Its absence had exposed the blocked door—and the delay had kept eight hundred people from breathing contaminated air. The concert began an hour late, to grateful silence.
