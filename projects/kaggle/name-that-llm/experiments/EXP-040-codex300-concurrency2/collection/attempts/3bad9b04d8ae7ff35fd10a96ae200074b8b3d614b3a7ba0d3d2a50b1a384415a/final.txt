The claim is too absolute. A good explanation should avoid unnecessary technical vocabulary, but it should not always avoid it. Specialized terms can make an explanation more precise, efficient, and faithful to the subject. For example, explaining a medical diagnosis without words such as “infection,” “inflammation,” or “antibiotic” may produce vague or misleading descriptions. Similarly, terms like “algorithm” or “interest rate” can be useful because they name ideas that would otherwise require long, awkward paraphrases. The important issue is not whether technical vocabulary appears, but whether the audience can understand and use it. An effective explanation introduces essential terms clearly, defines them in ordinary language, and connects them to examples or familiar experiences. It also avoids jargon that adds prestige without adding meaning. A physics teacher may need to use “gravity,” but should explain what it does; a lawyer may need “liability,” but should clarify who is responsible and why. Technical language can also help learners join a field’s real conversation, since they will encounter those terms in textbooks, workplaces, and discussions. Therefore, clarity requires thoughtful adaptation, not a blanket ban on specialized words.
