Mara returned from the marsh with a memory card full of victories: herons lifted like folded gray maps from the reeds, dragonflies hovered in jeweled suspension, and the sunset turned every puddle copper. She spent the evening sorting through the files, delighted by sharp wings, clean reflections, and one unlikely portrait of a fox watching from the path. Only one frame was ruined. It showed nothing but a streak of pale gold, dark water, and a crooked flare of sky. She had pressed the shutter while turning, startled by a laugh behind her, and the camera had caught the world slipping sideways. Her friends advised deleting it. “You have a hundred better ones,” they said. Mara agreed, almost, but kept it. Months later, the fox photograph won a small local prize and the heron appeared in a calendar, yet the blurred image stayed pinned above her desk. In it she could still hear her father’s laugh, bright and sudden, as he called her name from the boardwalk. He had been well that afternoon, walking without his cane, teasing her for photographing birds that refused to pose. The blur was not beautiful in any conventional way, but it held the instant before she turned to see him smiling.
