The evidence suggests that the feature is attractive in principle but has not yet demonstrated durable value in practice. Enthusiastic comments are useful signals of interest, emotional appeal, or initial comprehension, but they may reflect novelty, politeness, excitement about the idea, or a desire to support the team rather than a behavior users consider important enough to repeat. Low repeat usage should therefore be treated as a stronger warning about product-market fit, usefulness, usability, or workflow integration. The team should examine activation and retention by user cohort, identify where people stop returning, and compare stated enthusiasm with observed behavior. Follow-up interviews should ask what users expected, what job they tried to accomplish, what prevented a second use, and whether the feature solved a recurring problem. The team should avoid concluding either that the feature is a success or that it should be abandoned. Instead, it should test specific hypotheses about friction, frequency of need, discoverability, and value, then measure whether targeted improvements increase meaningful repeat usage.
