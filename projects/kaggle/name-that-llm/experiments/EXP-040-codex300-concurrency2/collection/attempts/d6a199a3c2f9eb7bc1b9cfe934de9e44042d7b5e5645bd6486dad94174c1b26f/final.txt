Every evening, Mara practiced her cello in the municipal hall, facing three hundred empty seats. She liked their silence. Empty seats never checked their watches when she stumbled over the same passage, never suggested she might be happier teaching. At nine, somewhere beyond the doors, a bucket rattled. At ten, the lights in the foyer went out. For weeks, these sounds were part of her rehearsal. Then one Thursday she stopped before the final movement, too tired to continue. From the back of the hall came a small, disappointed sigh. A man stood beside the last row, one hand resting on a mop. “Sorry,” he said. “I thought you were taking a breath.” Mara lowered her bow. “How long have you been there?” He glanced toward the doors. “Since they changed my shift. November.” It was February. “You listen every night?” “Not Tuesdays. You do scales on Tuesdays.” She laughed, and he smiled without looking at her. After a moment he said, “That bit you keep starting again. It sounded better yesterday.” No one had heard enough of her playing lately to say such a thing. Mara settled the cello against her chest. “Will you stay for the ending?” He parked his bucket outside and took a seat.
