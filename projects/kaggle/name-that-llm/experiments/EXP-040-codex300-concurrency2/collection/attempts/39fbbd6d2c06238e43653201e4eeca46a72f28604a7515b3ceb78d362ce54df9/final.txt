Collecting objects as souvenirs is primarily a personal, emotional practice: an item is valued because it recalls a trip, relationship, achievement, or moment in the collector’s life. Its meaning may be intimate and selective, depending on memory, sentiment, beauty, or novelty rather than on whether the object can establish facts. A shell from a holiday beach, a concert ticket, or a family heirloom can function as a souvenir even if its date, origin, and wider significance are uncertain. Collecting objects as historical evidence has a different purpose. The object is treated as a source from which people can investigate the past, asking who made it, used it, preserved it, and under what social, economic, or political conditions. Provenance, date, material, context of discovery, and comparison with other sources become essential because they affect what claims the item can support. A soldier’s letter, for example, may be emotionally treasured by descendants, but historians may also analyze it for evidence about wartime experience, literacy, censorship, or attitudes. The same object can serve both roles, yet the approaches differ: souvenir collecting centers on personal remembrance, while historical collecting requires careful documentation, contextual interpretation, and awareness that objects may reveal partial, biased, or contested versions of the past.
