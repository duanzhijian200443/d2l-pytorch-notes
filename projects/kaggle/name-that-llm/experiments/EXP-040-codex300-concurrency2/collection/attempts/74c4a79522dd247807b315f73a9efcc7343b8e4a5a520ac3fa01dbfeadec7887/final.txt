A city’s street names can serve as an unofficial historical record because each naming decision reflects what people in power, and sometimes the wider community, consider worth recognizing. Older names may preserve traces of vanished landscapes, local trades, or religious institutions: Mill Road, Orchard Lane, and Church Street suggest how earlier residents worked and organized their surroundings. As cities expand, names honoring industrialists, military leaders, or national politicians can reveal growing admiration for commerce, empire, or statehood. Later changes may signal a shift in values. Renaming a road associated with colonial rule after an independence leader, for example, makes a political transformation visible in everyday life. Streets dedicated to civil rights activists, women, or Indigenous figures can indicate efforts to broaden public recognition and challenge earlier exclusions. Yet this record is neither complete nor neutral. Developers may choose prestigious or picturesque names to attract buyers, while residents may resist renaming because familiar addresses anchor personal memories. Old and new names therefore coexist, exposing tensions between inherited identity and present aspirations. Read together, street signs form a layered public archive: they show not simply who lived in a city, but whom successive generations chose to celebrate, overlook, or reconsider.
