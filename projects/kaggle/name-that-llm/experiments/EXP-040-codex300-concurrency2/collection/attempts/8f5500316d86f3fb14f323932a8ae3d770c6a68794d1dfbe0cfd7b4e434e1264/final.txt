The claim that a good explanation should always avoid technical vocabulary is too absolute. Technical terms can create barriers when readers do not know them, especially if simpler words would communicate the same idea. An explanation of inflation for a general audience, for example, is usually clearer when it begins with rising prices and reduced purchasing power rather than specialized economic terminology. However, avoiding technical vocabulary altogether can make an explanation vague, lengthy, or inaccurate. Specialized terms often exist because they identify concepts precisely. In medicine, law, engineering, and science, words such as “antibiotic,” “liability,” or “photosynthesis” may be essential to understanding the subject and participating in further discussion. The real issue is not whether technical vocabulary appears, but whether it is appropriate for the audience and adequately explained. A good explanation can introduce an unfamiliar term in ordinary language, give an example, and then use the term consistently. This approach both clarifies the immediate idea and helps the reader develop useful knowledge. Experts may require technical vocabulary because excessive simplification can obscure important distinctions, while beginners may need definitions and analogies. Therefore, good explanations should avoid unnecessary jargon, not technical vocabulary itself. Clarity depends on precision, context, and audience awareness rather than a universal ban on specialized words.
