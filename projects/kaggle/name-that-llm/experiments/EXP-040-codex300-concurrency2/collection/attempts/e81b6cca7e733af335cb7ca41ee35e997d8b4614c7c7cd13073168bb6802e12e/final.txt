Oral history can preserve experiences that official records overlook by recording people’s memories, interpretations, emotions, and everyday experiences in their own words. Government documents, newspapers, institutional reports, and legal records often emphasize leaders, policies, and major events, while neglecting women, workers, migrants, Indigenous communities, minorities, and people affected by decisions made elsewhere. Interviews can reveal how historical events shaped family life, work, identity, displacement, discrimination, or community relationships, providing details that may never have been written down. They can also challenge official narratives by presenting perspectives that institutions ignored or deliberately excluded. However, researchers must recognize that oral testimony is not a perfectly transparent record of the past. Memory can fade, change over time, or be influenced by later events, personal beliefs, trauma, cultural expectations, and the interviewer’s questions. People may unintentionally misremember dates or combine separate experiences, while some may remain silent about painful or sensitive subjects. Therefore, oral histories should be treated as valuable evidence of both past experiences and later memory, interpreted carefully alongside other sources rather than accepted uncritically.
