Every evening, after the orchestra had gone home, Mara remained alone beneath the dim chandeliers of the concert hall, practicing the same difficult passage on her violin. The empty seats seemed stern and endless, swallowing each mistake before returning it as a faint echo. She believed no one heard her struggles: the trembling high notes, the frustrated sighs, the moments when she lowered her bow and whispered that she would never be ready. Then, one night, she finished the passage without faltering and heard a soft clap from the back row. Startled, Mara turned and saw Tomas, the cleaner, standing beside his cart with his hands raised apologetically. He confessed that he had listened every evening while polishing the aisles, always careful not to disturb her. “At first, the music sounded frightened,” he said, “but each night it grew braver.” Mara laughed, embarrassed, and asked why he had never spoken before. Tomas shrugged. “It seemed like a private conversation between you and the hall.” From then on, she played differently. The seats no longer felt empty, and the echoes no longer sounded cruel. Each evening, Tomas worked quietly in the shadows, and Mara practiced toward the single light left burning in the back row, knowing that even unfinished music could matter to someone.
