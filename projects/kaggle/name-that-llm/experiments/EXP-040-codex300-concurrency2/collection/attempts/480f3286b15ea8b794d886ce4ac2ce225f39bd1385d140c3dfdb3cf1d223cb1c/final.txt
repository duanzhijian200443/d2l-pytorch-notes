Every evening, after the last audience member had gone and the theater lights were dimmed, Mara practiced alone beneath the faded golden balconies. Her violin filled the empty hall with hesitant scales, then rose into the aching melody she had never managed to play without mistakes. She assumed no one heard her except the dust and the old velvet seats. One night, as she packed her instrument, a broom tapped softly against the floor. Near the rear doors stood the cleaner, a quiet man named Elias, holding his cart and smiling. “I’m sorry,” he said. “I didn’t mean to intrude.” Mara noticed his eyes were shining. He told her he had been listening every evening for three months, pausing his work whenever she played that melody. His wife had loved it, he explained, and hearing it again made the long nights bearable. Mara looked at the scuffed floor, embarrassed by every wrong note he must have heard. “You stayed even when I failed?” she asked. Elias leaned on his broom. “Especially then,” he replied. “It sounded like someone refusing to give up.” The next evening, Mara left the doors open. When she began to play, Elias stopped sweeping, and for the first time, the empty hall did not feel empty at all.
