The shop could reserve a fixed portion of each day for appointments and leave the remaining capacity for walk-in customers. For example, technicians might accept appointments in two-hour blocks, with a limited number of same-day bookings, while protecting one service bay and some technician time for walk-ins. Walk-ins would be served in the order they arrive, subject to safety and job complexity, and staff should provide realistic estimated wait times. Customers with appointments should receive a reasonable grace period, such as 10 minutes; after that, the slot may be given to a waiting walk-in if the customer has not called. The shop could also use a cancellation list to fill openings and offer text notifications when an earlier slot becomes available. Routine, predictable repairs should generally require appointments, while inspections, flat tires, minor adjustments, and urgent problems could be accepted as walk-ins when capacity allows. The main compromise is flexibility versus certainty: reserving capacity for walk-ins improves access for unexpected needs but means appointment customers may face longer waits or fewer available times, while prioritizing appointments makes scheduling more reliable but can leave technicians idle during no-shows and turn away customers who need immediate help. A clearly posted policy, consistent triage, and honest wait-time estimates can keep the arrangement fair.
