The shop could reserve about 70 percent of each day’s labor capacity for scheduled appointments and leave 30 percent available for walk-ins, adjusting the ratio over time based on demand. Appointments would receive firm check-in windows and priority for diagnosis and repair, while walk-ins would be accepted on a first-come, first-served basis until the day’s flexible capacity is full. At check-in, staff should give walk-in customers a realistic estimated start time and offer alternatives such as leaving the vehicle, joining a text-message waitlist, or booking the next available appointment. To protect the schedule, urgent safety problems could be triaged immediately, but only brief inspections or truly critical repairs should interrupt appointment work. If appointment customers arrive late, their jobs could be moved into the walk-in queue or rescheduled, preventing one delay from disrupting the entire day. The main compromise is between predictability and accessibility. Reserving capacity for appointments gives customers reliable service times and helps technicians plan parts, bays, and workloads, but it can leave resources idle when appointments are canceled. Holding capacity for walk-ins attracts customers who need immediate help and keeps the shop flexible, but it reduces the number of guaranteed bookings and can create uncertain waits. Regularly reviewing arrival patterns, cancellations, repair duration, and customer feedback can help the shop refine the balance.
