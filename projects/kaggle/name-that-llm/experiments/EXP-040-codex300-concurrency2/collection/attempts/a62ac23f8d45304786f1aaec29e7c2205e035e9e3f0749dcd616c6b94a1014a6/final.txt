Ocean water does not simply collect permanently on the side facing the Moon because Earth and the Moon are orbiting their common center of mass, so Earth as a whole is continually falling toward the Moon rather than being held still beneath it. What produces tides is not the Moon’s total gravitational pull but the difference in that pull across Earth. Water on the Moon-facing side is attracted slightly more strongly than Earth’s center, creating a bulge toward the Moon. On the far side, the Moon’s attraction is slightly weaker than at Earth’s center, so that water is left relatively farther from the Moon, creating a second bulge. As Earth rotates beneath these roughly Moon-aligned bulges, most coastlines pass through regions of higher and lower water, usually producing two high tides and two low tides per lunar day. The water cannot adjust instantly or form perfectly smooth bulges because continents block its motion, ocean basins have different shapes and depths, and friction, currents, and Earth’s rotation alter the response. The Sun also raises tides and can strengthen or weaken lunar tides depending on its alignment with Earth and the Moon. Thus tides are moving, repeating deformations of the oceans driven mainly by differential gravity, orbital motion, and Earth’s rotation, not a permanent heap of water pulled to one side.
