Oral history preserves experiences absent from official records by recording the memories, interpretations, emotions, and everyday knowledge of people who witnessed or participated in past events. Government files, newspapers, and institutional archives often emphasize powerful organizations and public decisions, while overlooking private life, informal labor, family relationships, local traditions, and the perspectives of marginalized communities. Interviews can therefore reveal how policies and conflicts were experienced on the ground, preserve voices that were excluded from written documentation, and show how individuals understood change in their own terms. They may also capture tone, hesitation, storytelling practices, and community values that written sources cannot fully convey. However, researchers should remember that oral testimony is shaped by memory, later experiences, social expectations, and the interview setting. People may forget details, combine events, or reinterpret the past over time. This does not make oral history useless, but it means testimony should be critically contextualized and, when possible, compared with other interviews, documents, material evidence, and relevant historical scholarship.
