Asking users to confirm a destructive action twice can be helpful when the consequences are serious, irreversible, or difficult to recover from, such as permanently deleting an account, erasing important data, or sending a large payment. The first prompt can interrupt an accidental click, while the second can require the user to reconsider after seeing a clear description of what will happen. This pattern is most effective when the confirmations are meaningfully different: for example, the first explains the consequences and the second asks the user to type the account name or deletion phrase. It is also useful when the action is unusual, high-impact, or performed in a context where mistakes are likely. However, repeated confirmation becomes ineffective when users encounter it frequently or when both prompts look and sound identical. People quickly learn to click through familiar warnings, especially when they are trying to complete routine work. Excessive confirmations create friction, encourage habituation, and may even reduce safety by training users to ignore important messages. They can also frustrate users who understand the consequences and make the interface feel distrustful or poorly designed. Confirmation should therefore be proportional to risk, placed at the moment of commitment, written in specific language, and paired with safer alternatives such as undo, recovery periods, or reversible archiving whenever possible.
