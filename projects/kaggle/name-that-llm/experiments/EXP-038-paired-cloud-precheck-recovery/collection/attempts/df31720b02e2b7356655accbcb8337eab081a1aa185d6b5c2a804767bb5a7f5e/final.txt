A puddle can disappear on a cool day because evaporation happens at temperatures far below boiling. Water molecules are constantly moving, and some at the surface have enough energy to escape into the air as vapor. The fastest molecules leave first, gradually reducing the puddle’s volume. Warmth increases evaporation, but it is not required; even cool water contains molecules with a range of energies. Dry air, sunlight, wind, and a large exposed surface can make evaporation faster. Dry air can absorb more water vapor, while wind carries moist air away and replaces it with drier air. Boiling, in contrast, occurs throughout the liquid when vapor bubbles form, whereas evaporation occurs only at the surface and can slowly remove water at almost any temperature.
